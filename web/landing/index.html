saturnus: classic HP calculators in your browser

saturnus emulates the HP 48SX, HP 48GX, HP 49G, HP 38G, HP 39G, HP 40G, HP 42S, HP 27S, HP 17BII, HP 17B, HP 28S and HP 19BII. Use it in the browser, as a desktop app or from the command line.

Open saturnus Desktop and command line

Free and open source (MIT). Nothing is uploaded: the ROM and your work stay on your device. No ROM is included; get one from hpcalc.org.

Twelve calculators

HP's graphing, scientific and business calculators from 1988 to 2000, each running its own ROM. Pick one to read what was new about it.

  • HP 48SX 1990

    The 28S's RPL in a case with two card ports, a serial port and infrared. Owners used that serial port to share programs online, and many did.

  • HP 48GX 1993

    Input forms and choose lists in every application, and the Equation Library built in: over 300 equations in 15 subjects. HP sold it for about ten years.

  • HP 49G 1999

    Computer algebra built in, written mainly by Bernard Parisse, with algebraic or RPN entry. Its ROM sits in flash memory, so you can update it yourself.

  • HP 38G 1995

    A graphing calculator for school maths and science, designed with eight teachers. A teacher builds an aplet and beams it to the whole class by infrared.

  • HP 39G 2000

    The 38G's successor, with eight times the memory on the same screen. New aplets test hypotheses and explore trigonometry and quadratics.

  • HP 40G 2000

    The 39G with the 49G's computer algebra. It trades the infrared for a cable to a PC and to other calculators.

  • HP 42S 1988

    It has all the programmable functions of the HP-41, so HP-41 programs run on it. A two-line display, menus, complex numbers, matrices and a solver.

  • HP 27S 1988

    HP's first algebraic scientific: menus instead of RPN. A solver for typed equations, statistics, money maths and a clock with appointments, but no programming.

  • HP 17BII 1990

    Business maths in RPN or algebraic; with the 19BII, it was the first HP to offer both. Cash flows, bonds, depreciation and a solver for your own formulas.

  • HP 17B 1988

    Business maths with menus and prompts: money over time, cash flows, bonds and depreciation. Its solver takes your own equations instead of a program.

  • HP 28S 1988

    Symbolic algebra and calculus in a case that opens like a book. It followed the 28C, the first calculator with computer algebra, and has 16 times its memory.

  • HP 19BII 1990

    HP's last clamshell: a business calculator with letter keys and a four-line display. Solver, money maths, appointments, text lists and six languages.

HP 48SX 1990

The 28S's RPL, with card ports, infrared and a serial port.

Launch price $350

Introduced
March 1990
Language
RPL
Display
131 × 64 pixels
Memory
32 KB RAM (up to 288 KB with cards), 256 KB ROM
Ports
Two card slots, a 3-wire serial port, two-way infrared

What was new

  • The RPL of the HP 28 (symbolic maths, named variables, structured programs) in a case with expansion ports.
  • The EquationWriter and the MatrixWriter: formulas and matrices entered as they look on paper.
  • Units that work throughout the system, the solver included.
  • Two-way infrared between calculators, and Kermit over the serial port.

Its story

The card slots took RAM cards and many application cards, such as HP's Solve Equation Library card with more than 300 equations. The infrared range was kept short at teachers' request.

The serial port arrived as the internet spread. Owners shared programs in large numbers, in the comp.sys.hp48 newsgroup and on many web sites.

HP 48GX 1993

Input forms, and the Equation Library built in.

Launch price $350

Introduced
June 1993
Sold
1993 to 2003
Memory
128 KB RAM, 512 KB ROM
Port 1
Cards up to 128 KB, which can be merged with user memory
Port 2
Cards up to 4 MB, seen as 128 KB ports 2 to 32

What was new

  • Input forms and choose lists throughout the applications.
  • The Equation Library built in: more than 300 equations in 15 subjects. On the 48SX it was a card.
  • Four times the RAM of the 48SX and twice the ROM.

Its story

The 48GX was sold for about ten years, from 1993 to 2003. By 1999 its price had come down from $350 to $179.95.

Its second port took cards of up to 4 MB, of which 3968 KB could be used. The calculator showed them as 128 KB ports, numbered 2 to 32.

HP 49G 1999

Computer algebra built in, and a ROM you can update yourself.

Launch price $180

Announced
21 May 1999, at the OpenHP conference in Paris
Entry
Algebraic or RPN
Memory
512 KB RAM, 2 MB flash (1 MB ROM, 1 MB for you)
Ports
10-wire serial; no infrared, no card slots
Case
Ice blue, rubber keys

What was new

  • A computer algebra system built in, with step-by-step solving.
  • The ROM sits in flash memory, so owners can update it themselves.
  • Algebraic or RPN entry.
  • Cut, copy and paste, and find and replace.

Its story

Bernard Parisse wrote most of the computer algebra, from his Erable library, with code from ALG48 by Fiechter and Heiskanen inside.

HP said the 49G had three times the memory of other calculators on sale at the time. It also left things out: the 48's infrared, its card slots and the Equation Library.

HP 38G 1995

A graphing calculator designed with eight teachers.

Launch price $79.95

Introduced
April 1995
Entry
Algebraic, not RPN
Display
131 × 64 pixels
Memory
32 KB RAM, 512 KB ROM
Links
Two-way infrared, RS-232 to a PC

What was new

  • Aplets: small applications for one topic each, seen in three views: symbolic, graphic and numeric.
  • Aplets built in for functions, parametric and polar curves, sequences, statistics and solving.
  • A split screen, implied multiplication and a numeric table view.

Its story

HP made the 38G for maths and science in secondary school, with an advisory committee of eight teachers. A teacher builds an aplet and beams it to the students by infrared, so the whole class starts from the same setup.

Its software is based on the 48G's. It has a sliding cover, and an accessory put its screen on an overhead projector.

HP 39G 2000

The 38G's successor, with eight times the memory.

Launch price $79.99

Introduced
2000
Display
131 × 64 pixels, as on the 38G
Memory
256 KB RAM
Links
Two-way infrared; prints to HP infrared printers

None

What was new

  • The Inference aplet: hypothesis tests and confidence intervals.
  • The Trig Explorer and the Quadratic Explorer.
  • A memory manager.
  • Values read straight off a plot.

Its story

The 39G follows the 38G. The screen stayed the same size; the RAM grew from 32 KB to 256 KB.

39Gs pass aplets, lists and matrices to each other by infrared. It has no computer algebra: that came with the 40G.

HP 42S 1988

The HP-41's functions, on a two-line display.

Launch price $120

Introduced
October 1988
Discontinued
1995 or 1996
Entry
RPN
Display
Two lines of 22 characters (131 × 16 pixels)
Memory
About 7.2 KB
Input and output
Infrared printing to the HP 82240A, nothing else

What was new

  • All the programmable functions of the HP-41C and 41CV, so their programs run (keyed in by hand: there is no card reader). Not every 41CX function is there.
  • Menus, and custom menus of your own.
  • Complex numbers, in polar notation too, and vectors.
  • Matrices with an editor and a linear solver; a solver, numeric integration and base arithmetic.

Its story

The 42S was made until 1995 or 1996. Its design lives on in SwissMicros' DM42 of 2017. The DM42 runs Free42, an independent re-implementation, not a copy of HP's ROM.

HP 27S 1988

HP's first algebraic scientific: menus, no RPN.

Launch price $110

Introduced
January 1988
Entry
Algebraic, no RPN
Menus
Six menu keys
Programming
None: equations go into the solver

What was new

  • HP's first scientific calculator with algebraic entry.
  • A solver for the equations you type in (HP Solve).
  • Statistics, time value of money and base conversions.
  • A clock with appointments, and date arithmetic.

Its story

HP launched the 27S together with the 17B, the 19B and the 28S, all four with HP Solve and printing by infrared. It belongs to HP's Pioneer family and shares its hardware with the 17B.

It has no keystroke programming: equations go into the solver instead.

Business maths with a solver for your own equations.

Launch price $110

Introduced
January 1988

Algebraic only, in chain order (no precedence)
Display
Two lines, with menus and prompts
Memory
6.5 KB
Printing
By infrared

What was new

  • A two-line display with menus and prompts.
  • A solver for your own equations, which the manual calls easier than programming.
  • A clock with appointments.

Its story

HP launched the 17B in January 1988 with the 27S, the 19B and the 28S. It handles time value of money, cash flows, bonds, depreciation and statistics.

It works in chain order: 2 + 3 × 4 gives 20, not 14. In 1990 the 17BII replaced it and added RPN.

HP 28S 1988

Symbolic algebra in a case that opens like a book.

Launch price $235

Introduced
January 1988
Discontinued
1992
Language
RPL
Memory
32 KB RAM (the 28C had 2 KB)
Case
Opens like a book; a letter keyboard on the left half

What was new

  • 32 KB of RAM instead of the 28C's 2 KB.
  • Directories to organise your variables.
  • Graphics objects.

Its story

Its forerunner, the 28C of January 1987, was the first calculator with computer algebra. Most of its operating system was written in RPL.

The 28S does symbolic algebra and calculus, HP Solve, complex numbers, vectors, matrices, plots, units, statistics and binary arithmetic. The calculator is its own case: it opens and closes like a book. Its RPL went on into the HP 48.

What you can do

  • Type and paste RPL

    Paste a program and the app types it into the command line, key by key. Your keyboard's letters, digits and Enter go to the calculator; F1 to F6 are the menu keys.

  • Look inside the memory

    The memory view shows the variables, directories, stack and flags, read live from the calculator's RAM. Store, rename, purge, set a flag: the calculator does it itself.

  • Edit programs

    Open a variable or a stack level in an RPL editor with highlighting, indentation and completion. The calculator compiles what you save, so a syntax error is its own message.

  • Look up any command

    Search (Cmd+K or Ctrl+K) holds a reference of every model's commands, with examples run in saturnus. Enter runs the command on the calculator; on the 38G, 39G and 40G it goes on the edit line for you to finish.

  • Keep your work

    The calculator keeps its memory across reloads, as a real one does when switched off. Save and load states, and copy or save the screen as a PNG.

  • Real speed, or faster

    Programs take as long as on the real calculator, within a few percent. Switch to 2×, 4× or Max for a long one.

The memory view, the editor and pasting work on the 48SX, 48GX and 49G.

Desktop app

macOS on Apple silicon, Windows and Linux on x86_64. The app downloads a model's ROM from hpcalc.org for you, after asking.

Download from the releases page

On a Mac also with Homebrew, see the install lines below. The Mac app is signed and notarised. Windows will ask once before the first start; the release notes say what to click.

Command line

Run a ROM with a key script, save the screen, serve the serial port for Kermit, or drive the calculator from a script.

Install lines below

Also as archives, .deb and .rpm on the releases page (the package saturnus; the desktop app's is saturnus-app).

# Command line: Rust
cargo install saturnus
# Command line: macOS and Linux, Homebrew
brew trust --formula ractive/tap/saturnus
brew install ractive/tap/saturnus
# Command line: Windows, Scoop
scoop bucket add ractive https://github.com/ractive/scoop-bucket
scoop install saturnus
# Desktop app: macOS, Homebrew
brew trust --cask ractive/tap/saturnus-app
brew install --cask ractive/tap/saturnus-app

The ROMs are not included

saturnus runs the calculator's own ROM, which is HP's software. hpcalc.org hosts the ROM files with HP's permission for use with emulators, and saturnus does not host or pass them on. Download the ROM for your model there, unzip it and drop the file into the app; the desktop app and saturnus rom fetch download it for you. The 42S, 27S, 17BII, 17B and 28S ROMs were never published, but you can read the ROM out of your own calculator with an HP 48 as the infrared receiver: Christoph Gießelink's Emu42 page points to the tools and the steps. The 19BII's was never published either and cannot be read out: saturnus runs it only from a file you already have.

Files: to and from a calculator

Files moves files between a calculator and your computer: an emulated one running in the app, or a real HP 48 or 49 on a serial cable (in Chrome or Edge on a computer). Copy files both ways, back up the whole memory, or serve a folder to a 38G, 39G or 40G. Files is part of the app, next to the calculators.

satx, on the command line

satx sends and receives files over a serial cable with an HP 48 or 49, by Kermit or XModem. It talks to saturnus the same way.

# Rust
cargo install satx
# macOS and Linux, Homebrew
brew trust --formula ractive/tap/satx
brew install ractive/tap/satx
# Windows, Scoop
scoop bucket add ractive https://github.com/ractive/scoop-bucket
scoop install satx

Also as archives, .deb and .rpm on the releases page (the package satx).

Written from the documentation

saturnus was written from HP's manuals, the hardware literature and the behaviour of the ROMs themselves, not from other emulators' source code. Every fact it relies on is in a public knowledge base with its source: the Saturn CPU, the display, the timers, the serial port, the memory layout and the transfer protocols.
